\begin{abstract}
  % scale
  EMVCo reports over 15.6 billion EMV chip cards are in use.
  Each chip-card authenticates itself to readers, often the in-store payment terminals, through an \emph{offline data authentication} (ODA) step.
  ODA requires the card chip to sign a fresh challenge from the reader, with a key that an RSA certificate chain ties to the certification authority (CA).
  % zkEMV
  We show that ODA already gives us a privacy-preserving credential when paired with zero-knowledge cryptography, without any change to card chip, issuers or payment networks.
  In \emph{zkEMV}, the user taps their contactless card on their own NFC-capable device, which participates in ODA.
  The captured transcript of communication over NFC is used for locally proving that a genuine EMV card signed a challenge "nonce" presented by the verifier, and that its issuer and card certificates are not expired.
  % verifier pov
  The verifier receives a zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) which it can instantly verify against root of trust CA public key without ever needing to see the card number, expiry dates or none of its certificates.
  The ZKP carries a verifier-scoped 32-byte nullifier hash that is linked to the card's ICC public key, resisting Sybil attack vector.
  On verifier's request the client can selectively disclose some attributes derivable from the card's bank identification number (BIN), i.e., first 6 to 8 digits of card number, such as, card issuing country, bank or nature of funding.
  % perf
  Proving runs entirely on user-owned device: when a Samsung Galaxy S25 Ultra is running on battery power, it takes 700ms for a Visa card and 800ms for a Mastercard, to prove ODA in zero-knowledge using Provekit, which implements a Spartan-based sumcheck protocol with WHIR polynomial commitment scheme.
  Verification of zkEMV proof takes under 100ms and proof size ranges in 620-640KiB.
  % summarise
  The talk presents the zkEMV protocol, with optimizations for sub-second latency client-side proving of ODA, followed by demonstrating practical use cases it opens doors to, which preserves user privacy and reduces merchant liability when handling personally identifiable informations (PIIs).
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

% problem
Any online business providing a free-tier service, such as AI inference tokens, GPU compute credits, privacy-focused virtual private network (VPN) or email relayer, faces abuse from spammers, often degrading the quality of service (QoS) for real customers.
Stripe reports that 7.4\% of sign-ups at AI company websites are implicated in suspected multi-account abuse~\cite{stripe-abuse}.
This is the classic Sybil attack~\cite{douceur-sybil}: without a trusted authority certifying identities, one user can always pose as many.

% solution
Service providers resort to collecting user identifiables to stop spammers from easily creating new identities.
The usual defence mechanism includes verify customer phone number, upload Govt. issued identity card, or add a payment card.
These help service providers deduplicate user accounts and limit resource usage to only unique users.
For sake of convenience, major card networks such Visa and Mastercard offer tap-to-verify products~\cite{visa-tap,mc-tap-to-verify}.
% new problem
But it opens door to a much bigger problem: service providers have the responsibility of carefully handling any customer data which falls under the umbrella of PII, as they become a lucrative target for hackers.
Then there are privacy-concious customers who are aware of these potential data breaches and they prefer not to put their card number, expiry dates or CVV on a random website, which leaves them unable to try out a product or service.

Surprisingly each chip-based card already holds necessary cryptographic foundation to build a much more privacy-respecting authentication mechanism.
% no ZK
During ODA, a contactless card sends the reader a certificate chain with a fresh challenge signed with the private key counterpart, it holds inside the chip~\cite{emv-book2}.
These credentials are already issued and in circulation, almost 15.6 billion EMV chip cards are in use~\cite{emvco-stats}, contrary to Privacy Pass~\cite{privacypass} and Private Access Tokens~\cite{apple-pat} which rely on a platform attester.
% problem
The EMV certificates however embed the card number, which is formally known as primary account number (PAN), so verifying the certificate chain server-side means handing over user identifiables.
% with ZK
Zero-knowledge cryptography comes to a rescue: produce a zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) on user-device over the certificate chain without exposing the PAN.
% differentiation
Zero-knowledge proofs over existing credentials, such as mdoc with ECDSA~\cite{frigo-shelat}, JWT and mDL~\cite{crescent}, OpenID Connect~\cite{zklogin} and legacy signatures~\cite{legacy-sigs}, need enrolment with a government or a trusted identity provider.
To the best of our knowledge, no earlier work presents zero-knowledge proving of EMV ODA with practical use cases it leads to.

\section{The protocol}

There are two participants in the protocol: a user (prover) and a service provider (verifier).
User has a near field communication (NFC) capable device and an EMV card in their physical possession.
User proves to the service provider that they tapped a genuine EMV card on their device whose certificates have not expired as of verification time.

\paragraph{Flow.}
The verifier issues a challenge: a random 4-byte nonce, the current time at month-level granularity (in format \texttt{YY-MM}), a \emph{scope} and optionally set bits for revealing card attributes, derivable from BIN.
The user device fetches the challenge and ensures the requested scope is same as the origin authenticates to over TLS.
The user taps the EMV card on their device, which forwards the payload, i.e., nonce and time, for signing by the card-held ICC private key.
The card chip returns back the signature with both issuer and ICC certificate, which chains upto the root of trust anchor --- CA public key.
The captured transcript of NFC communication is fed into the circuit for proving on user device.
The proof outputs a 32-byte nullifier hash, i.e., Poseidon2~\cite{poseidon2} hash of card's certified ICC public key and verifier scope.
On receiving the ZKP, the verifier checks the proof's public inputs equal to its challenge and the CA key is what it trusts, followed by verifying the ZKP itself.

\paragraph{Statement.}
The public inputs to the circuit are CA public key, nonce, time, scope and optional bits set for revealing attributes (based on BIN) with a BIN-table Merkle root.
% cert chain
As depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:chain}, three RSA signatures are verified along the EMV certificate chain, such that the signed messages are recovered with $\texttt{msg} = \texttt{sig} ^ 3 \bmod n$, where $n$ is the RSA modulus.
Recovered messages are parsed following ISO 9796-2~\cite{iso9796-2} encoding with sufficient assertions.
Then it recomputes the SHA-1 hash~\cite{fips180-4} over the recovered message to enforce integrity.
Recovered message holds the next public key in certificate chain to continue with the authenticity check.
Additionally it enforces that the issuer identifier matches the card PAN prefix and both certificates are not expired compared to the verifier-chosen \texttt{YY-MM}.
% nullifier
To prevent a malicious user from proving forgable statements, the circuit computes predicates only from bytes which are covered by the signatures in the certificate chain.
The circuit-output nullifier is deterministic for an EMV card with a verifier-scoped challenge.
The scope is what dictates if the nullifier is linkable across verifiers: to prevent a malicious verifier from learning nullifier in another verifier's scope, the client only accepts challenge which originates from an authenticated scope.
% attributes
Optionally the circuit can reveal certain requested attributes without exposing the BIN, i.e., the card issuer country, whether it is credit, debit or prepaid card, brand name and whether or not for commercial use.
A binary Merkle tree, built with Poseidon2 hash function, over ranges of first 12 digits of PAN, is used for proving inclusion against the Merkle root hash, which the challenger commits to.

\section{Use cases}

zkEMV-based authentication helps anywhere fake accounts drain resources from real users and no actual payment needs to happen.
User does not need to enter their card details trusting a random website and the website database operator does not need to worry about hackers getting their hands on the card-data mine.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Free tiers and trials}
Daily quota of LLM token allowance for each account proving possession of a genuine EMV card without ever risking loss of customer data.
\item \textbf{Sticky banlists}
Communities on Discord which seek only well-mannered humans, may maintain a banlist with scoped nullifiers, without ever seeing user's card details or email address.
\item \textbf{One vote per card}
Polls and petitions allowing only one participation from one EMV card-owner, with nullifiers staying unlinkable across events preserve user privacy from curious platform operator.
\item \textbf{Two factor authentication}
Before letting any irreversible account operation to happen, user taps their EMV card on their phone to prove that it derives the enrolled nullifier.
\item \textbf{Bot friction}
To evade bots from spamming service, accepting zkEMV proof is a privacy-respecting alternative.
\end{itemize}

Additionally services might impose constraints such as "it needs to be a credit card issued in a specific country" without them learning the card number.
Anti-abuse aggregators such as the Human Passport~\cite{human-passport} could potentially accept a zkEMV proof as another signal.

\section{Client-side proving}

A zkEMV proof is generated on user's own device, without the card data ever leaving it.
The Noir~\cite{noir} circuits are proved using Provekit~\cite{provekit}, developed by World~\cite{world-provekit}.
Provekit generates "zero-knowledge" proofs using a Spartan-based~\cite{spartan} sumcheck protocol with WHIR~\cite{whir} hash-based polynomial commitment scheme, without any trusted setup.
Each distinct EMV card profile gets its own circuit due to differences in RSA key bit-length.

\section{Limitations}

A zkEMV proof shows that a genuinely issued card was live and tapped, but that does not necessarily prove "personhood" due to relative ease for one to possess multiple EMV cards.
Skipping authentication of verifier origin lets a malicious verifier obtain nullifier for another verifier's scope, which effectively lets the malicious party scan user's presence across venues, if nullifier database gets leaked.
Because Provekit proofs are zero-knowledge~\cite{world-provekit}, privacy of card data holds against verifiers, but not against card issuer network.
Any merchant which has already seen an EMV card tap, i.e., it has captured the card-specific ICC public key, can deduce its nullifiers, by bruteforcing verifier "scope".
zkEMV security model inherits EMV's ODA security: SHA-1 has seen chosen-prefix collision attack~\cite{sha1-shambles}, recently RSA-896 challenge number saw successful factoring surpassing previous records~\cite{weis-rsa896}, though EMVCo expects 1984-bit RSA keys to last until at least 2036~\cite{emvco-kl30}, and ISO/IEC 9796-2 has well-studied forgery attacks~\cite{cntw}.
EMVCo specifies the use of elliptic curve Schnorr digital signature algorithm (EC-SDSA) over NIST P-256 curve with SHA-256 hash function~\cite{emv-c8,emv-book-e}, but most issued contactless cards still rely on RSA for offline signatures~\cite{emvco-quantum}.
Even though with zkEMV a verifier does not see raw signatures over 4-byte nonce from the card-specific ICC key, if an attacker can harvest $k$ signatures from a card, it has the likelihood of $k \cdot 2^{-32}$ in successfully forging signatures on behalf of the card.

\section{Why RWC}
The talk brings a new credential class to RWC: one held by billions of people,  a live hardware challenge.
It is timely because the non-private version of card-tap verification is shipping now.
Attendees will see a working design that is honest about its limits, and techniques for proving legacy RSA, 9796-2 and SHA-1 in a hash-based prover on a phone, which carry over to other legacy credentials.
The talk also raises open questions for EMVCo, wallet vendors and regulators: next-generation card protocols, scheme rules on non-payment reads, and whether the nullifier counts as personal data.

zkEMV is open source under MIT or Apache-2.0~\cite{zkemv-repo}.
It comprises Noir circuits for Visa fDDA and Mastercard DDA, a Rust prover and verifier crate that handles challenges and CA keys, and benchmarks that prove and verify on an Android phone.
The card layouts were taken from captures of real Visa and Mastercard cards, whose chains verify natively under the production CA keys.
The circuits are tested and benchmarked on synthetic taps  the same layouts.
The talk will include a live demo of an Android app that reads a card over NFC and proves on the device.
